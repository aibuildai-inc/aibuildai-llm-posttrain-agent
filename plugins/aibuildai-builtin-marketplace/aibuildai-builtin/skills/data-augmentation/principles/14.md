# Principle 14: Blur (Gaussian / Motion)

## Invariance it assumes

f(Blur(x)) = f(x). The task is invariant to small optical or motion blur — the label should not depend on fine high-frequency detail below the blur radius.

## Mechanism

Convolve with a Gaussian kernel (isotropic defocus blur) or with a directional kernel (motion blur, simulating linear camera or object motion). Typical kernel radius 1–5 px. Motion blur additionally chooses a random direction for the kernel's major axis.

## When this invariance typically holds

- **Natural-scene classification with real defocus / motion at test time**: street scenes, sports, handheld photography.
- **Dermoscopy / low-zoom medical imaging** with handheld cameras.
- **Video frames** where motion blur is physically realistic.
- **Surveillance / dash-cam**: both defocus and motion blur are plausible test-time degradations.

## When this invariance breaks

- **Tasks depending on fine high-frequency detail**:
  - Thin-vessel / vessel-bifurcation segmentation.
  - Crack / defect detection in industrial inspection.
  - Micro-calcification detection in mammography.
  - Small-object detection where the object is only a few pixels wide. Blurring away the signal defeats the task.
- **Reflection-based or coherent imaging**:
  - Seismic reflection (amplitude and phase both carry signal; a spatial blur distorts phase relationships in an unphysical way).
  - Coherent SAR imaging. Blur is not the physical noise model of these sensors.
- **Controlled-acquisition imaging**: high-end microscopy, scientific imaging with calibrated PSF — the PSF is already known and corrected.
- **Static-scene domains** where motion blur specifically does not occur: top-down microscopy, slide histology, slide-mounted samples. Applying motion blur there teaches a noise model that does not exist at test.

## Parameter sensitivity

Blur kernel size is the critical knob. A 3×3 Gaussian with σ ≈ 0.5 is nearly invisible; an 11×11 Gaussian with σ ≈ 3 visibly erases fine structure. The correct kernel size is the largest one such that the blurred training examples still match a plausible test-time degradation.

## Gaussian vs motion blur

- **Gaussian blur**: isotropic defocus. Realistic on any domain where the optical system can be out of focus.
- **Motion blur**: directional. Realistic only on domains where the camera or subject can move during exposure.

These two should be enabled or disabled independently, not bundled. A still-scene domain that accepts Gaussian blur may reject motion blur.

## Common misconception

"Motion blur is a generic augmentation." Motion blur is specifically realistic only for domains with camera or subject motion. Applying it to static-scene or coherent-imaging domains teaches the model a noise model that does not exist at test.
