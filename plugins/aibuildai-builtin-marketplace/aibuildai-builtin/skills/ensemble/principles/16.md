# Principle 16: Cross-Architecture (Heterogeneous) Ensemble

**Space**: Model-space / meta.

**Source**: Krogh & Vedelsby 1995 (ambiguity decomposition, NIPS 7); general ensemble learning literature.

## Definition

Train members from **different model families** — for example, CNN + Transformer; gradient-boosted trees + MLP; U-Net + FPN + DeepLab — and combine them by any of the prediction-space aggregation rules: simple average, weighted average, rank average, or a stacked meta-learner.

## Mechanism

Different architectures have different **inductive biases** — the implicit assumptions they make about what functions are easy to represent. A CNN imposes translation equivariance through convolutions; a Transformer imposes long-range token interactions through attention; a tree model imposes axis-aligned decision boundaries. Members with different inductive biases make different mistakes on the same data. The Krogh-Vedelsby ambiguity decomposition makes this precise: for squared loss the ensemble error decomposes as

```
ensemble_error = average_member_error − ambiguity
```

where ambiguity is the mean squared disagreement among members. Heterogeneous ensembles typically have much larger ambiguity than same-family ensembles, so for fixed average member accuracy they achieve lower ensemble error.

## Pros

- **Usually the single most effective ensemble lever** near the performance ceiling, because architectural diversity produces genuinely decorrelated errors rather than correlated small variations.
- **Robust to distribution shift**: different architectures fail on different subsets, so at least one member tends to stay useful under shift.
- Complementary with all other ensemble techniques.

## Cons

- **Operational complexity multiplies**: multiple training codebases, preprocessing pipelines, hyperparameter searches, and checkpoint formats.
- Inference cost is the sum of member forwards plus aggregation.
- **Calibration mismatch** across architectures is common. Raw probabilities from a Transformer and a tree model can live on very different scales, which biases naive soft voting. Rank averaging or per-member temperature scaling addresses this.

## When to Use

When single-family accuracy has saturated and per-example error patterns show the families disagree meaningfully (check OOF correlation — correlations above ~0.95 indicate little diversity gain, below ~0.85 indicate strong complementarity). Particularly effective when dataset characteristics span multiple inductive biases, such as tabular data with engineered features + learned representations.

## Common Misconception

That "averaging two models always helps." It only helps when errors are **sufficiently decorrelated**. Averaging two strong but highly correlated models barely beats either alone because the ambiguity term is near zero. Diversity has to be checked, not assumed.

## Load-Bearing Quote

> "Empirically, ensembles tend to yield better results when there is a significant diversity among the models."
>
> — Wikipedia, *Ensemble learning*, summarizing the ambiguity decomposition literature
