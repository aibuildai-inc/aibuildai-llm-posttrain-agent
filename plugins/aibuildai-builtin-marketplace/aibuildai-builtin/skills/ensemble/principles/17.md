# Principle 17: Pitfalls That Silently Destroy Ensemble Gains

**Cross-cutting.** These failure modes apply across all four ensemble spaces and are the most common causes of "ensembling did not help" in practice. Most of the value of reading this playbook comes from avoiding them.

## 1. Averaging Uncalibrated Probabilities

Soft voting assumes member probabilities share a scale. Different loss functions, different temperatures, and different architectures rarely produce probabilities on a shared scale. Naive averaging then biases toward whichever member is systematically sharpest, and that member dominates the aggregate even when it is not the most accurate. Fix: calibrate members first (temperature scaling on a held-out set) or switch to rank averaging / hard voting when calibration is not achievable.

## 2. Aggregating Binary Masks Instead of Probability Maps

For dense prediction, thresholding each member's output to a 0/1 mask *before* aggregation is equivalent to majority voting on uncalibrated decisions. All information about confidence is destroyed. Averaging 20 binary masks gives nearly the same result as picking a single strong one; the ensemble gain collapses. Fix: always aggregate in probability space (float probability maps), then threshold once at the very end after aggregation and TTA are all applied.

## 3. TTA Transforms That Violate Task Symmetry

Vertical flip on natural images with a gravity prior (sky up, ground down). Rotation on OCR, digit, or character recognition. Horizontal flip on tasks that depend on left-right distinction (left/right lane, left/right hand, left/right medical view). All three actively degrade performance — the "augmented" predictions come from inputs the model has not been trained to handle. Fix: validate each TTA transform against a held-out metric individually before composing the TTA set; never assume the default flip-based TTA is universally safe.

## 4. K-Fold Ensemble When Retrain-on-All Dominates

On scarce data, the bias penalty of training each fold model on only (K−1)/K samples can exceed the variance reduction from averaging K members. In that regime a single model retrained on 100% of the data beats the K-way fold ensemble. The only reliable way to know which regime applies is to measure both on held-out data. Fix: always run a retrain-on-all baseline alongside the K-fold ensemble and compare.

## 5. Stacking with a Complex Meta-Learner

Deep or tree-based meta-learners overfit the narrow OOF feature matrix. The meta-learner has far fewer training examples than the base learners (the OOF matrix has N rows but only M base-model columns) and a complex meta-learner easily memorizes it. Fix: default the meta-learner to ridge regression, logistic regression, or a small constrained linear blend. Escalate only with explicit held-out evidence that complexity is needed.

## 6. Snapshot Ensembling on a Non-Cyclic Schedule

Snapshot ensembling's entire premise is that cyclic LR restarts push the optimizer into different basins. Under a standard cosine decay with no restarts, "snapshots" at different epochs are nearly the same model sliding through one basin and provide no real diversity. Fix: either adopt a cyclic schedule deliberately, or use a different method (SWA, last-K averaging, EMA) that does not require a schedule change.

## 7. Weighted Averaging Overfit to Validation

When OOF data is small relative to the number of members, the weight-fitting procedure can overfit the OOF metric and produce weights that generalize worse than equal weights. Especially common when using unconstrained linear regression or aggressive greedy selection with hundreds of model options. Fix: prefer simple discrete weights, constrain weights non-negative and sum-to-one, cap the number of model members, and cross-validate the weight-fitting step itself.

## 8. Member Overlap Not Tracked

Combining member A ("ResNet-50 trained on full data") with member B ("ResNet-50 trained on fold-0 held-out") double-counts shared training signal. The ensemble is effectively weighted toward the data A saw, and diversity is much smaller than the member count suggests. Fix: track which training examples each member actually saw, and treat members that trained on overlapping data as partially-correlated when choosing weights or counting ensemble size.

## 9. Single-Architecture Ensemble at the Saturation Ceiling

Once a single model family saturates a task, adding more members of the same family yields almost no gain — ambiguity approaches zero as members become nearly identical. Practitioners often respond by adding *more* members of the same family and wondering why nothing improves. Fix: when the marginal gain from an additional same-family member drops below a small threshold, switch to adding a member from a different family (cross-architecture, principle 16) before continuing.

## 10. Not Checking OOF Error Correlation Before Ensembling

Ensembling is only valuable when member errors are decorrelated. Combining members with 0.98 OOF correlation gives almost no gain regardless of how the combination is done. Fix: compute pairwise OOF correlation between model members as the *first* step of ensemble design, not the last. Reject members that correlate too highly with an existing member; invest in adding members that correlate at most ~0.85 with the current ensemble.
