# Principle 4: EMA (Exponential Moving Average of Weights)

**Space**: Weight-space. Produces a single model.

**Source**: PyTorch `torch.optim.swa_utils` docs (`get_ema_multi_avg_fn`); timm model-EMA; widely used in self-supervised learning (MoCo, BYOL), diffusion training, EfficientNet-style recipes.

## Definition

Maintain a **shadow copy** of the model whose parameters are an exponentially-decaying moving average of the training model's parameters. After every optimizer step, update the shadow with the rule `W_ema ← decay · W_ema + (1 − decay) · W_model`, with decay typically in [0.99, 0.9999]. The training model is optimized normally by SGD; only the shadow is averaged. At the end of training the shadow is deployed.

## Mechanism

EMA continuously tracks the training trajectory, giving more weight to recent iterates but never discarding earlier ones entirely. The decay constant controls the effective window: decay = 0.999 means the average is dominated by roughly the last 1000 steps. This smooths the noisy per-step weight trajectory, effectively landing in a flatter region than the online model at the moment training stops. Unlike SWA, EMA runs throughout training, updates every step, and does not require a modified LR schedule.

## Pros

- LR-schedule-agnostic — drops in on any optimizer with any schedule.
- Single model at inference; one forward pass.
- Central to many modern recipes as a *training mechanism*, not just a post-hoc step: teacher networks in self-supervised learning, target networks in Q-learning, moving-average targets in diffusion training.

## Cons

- Introduces a decay hyperparameter that materially affects results. Too slow (e.g. 0.9999) and the EMA lags too far behind; too fast (e.g. 0.99) and it behaves almost like the online model.
- Early in training the EMA lags the online model and gives worse evaluations. Using EMA for early-stopping decisions can be misleading.
- BN running statistics in the EMA shadow may be stale for the same reason as in last-K averaging; a recalibration pass helps.

## When to Use

Almost always safe to enable when training deep networks with any standard optimizer. Particularly valuable for recipes with short decay schedules or noisy gradient signals (efficient image classifiers, diffusion models), and mandatory for training architectures that structurally require a slow-moving target network (MoCo, BYOL, DINO, diffusion).

## Common Misconception

That EMA and SWA are the same because both average weights. The mechanisms differ: SWA is an **equal-weight** (uniform) average collected only during a designated phase under a modified LR schedule. EMA is an **exponentially-weighted** running average updated at every step, agnostic to LR schedule. PyTorch's `AveragedModel` exposes both via different `multi_avg_fn` choices, which is the clearest signal they are two variants of the same framework rather than a single method.
